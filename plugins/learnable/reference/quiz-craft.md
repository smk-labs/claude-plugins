# How to write a question the learner cannot game

A bad question measures nothing and inflates the profile, which then aims the whole course at the wrong gaps. Most quiz questions are bad in one of a few predictable ways. This file is how to avoid all of them.

## Never ask for a definition

"What is an index?" measures whether they read a glossary. Ask instead what happens, what breaks, what to do first, or which of these would actually fix it. A good question is answerable by reasoning from the scenario, by someone who has never heard the term, and unanswerable by someone who has only heard the term.

## Every option must be defensible

The single most common failure: the wrong options are written in a naive or exaggerated voice, so the learner eliminates them by tone alone and scores well while knowing nothing. Write every option as something a competent practitioner might actually say. No absolutes like always or never. No strawmen. No comic answers.

Make the difference between options a matter of nuance:

- right idea, wrong order
- right tool, wrong reason
- works, but hides the real cause
- true statement, irrelevant to this scenario
- the fix a mid-level person would reach for first

## Randomize position, vary the count

Correct answers must not cluster at the top. Move them around deliberately across a batch. For multi-select, vary how many are correct: sometimes one, sometimes three, sometimes all four, sometimes none. A fixed shape, like always exactly two correct, is a pattern the learner will find faster than you expect.

Assume the learner is test-wise. Many adults have spent years on standardized exams and read the shape of a question before they read its content.

## Raise difficulty with constraints, not with worse options

To make a question harder, add concrete detail to the scenario: the isolation level, the row count, the latency budget, the acceptable staleness, the traffic pattern, the team size. Never make it harder by making the distractors more confusing or the wording more slippery.

## Batch, and let them say they do not know

Ask three or four questions per batch, in one call. Between batches, say nothing: no feedback, no hints, no encouragement. Feedback mid-quiz changes later answers.

The picker always offers a free-text option, and a learner writing "I do not know this" is the most valuable answer in the whole quiz. Treat it as data, never as failure, and never talk them out of it.

## Close each batch with a self-report

After each batch of scenario questions, ask one multi-select question listing four groups of terms, and ask which groups they could explain to another person. Say explicitly that recognizing a name does not count.

This separates two things a scenario question cannot separate: knowing the vocabulary and being able to act. That distinction is the most useful line in the whole profile, because it decides whether a chapter teaches a concept or only names it.

## Calibrate in two phases

Run a first pass across the whole domain at moderate difficulty. Then run a second pass that goes harder where they scored well and easier where they struggled, to find the real ceiling and the real floor. A single-difficulty quiz finds neither.

## Interpret honestly

Score, then throw the score away. What matters is the shape:

- Where is recognition ahead of application, and by how much?
- Which wrong answer did they give twice in different clothes? That is a stable wrong model and it is worth a whole chapter.
- Where did a correct answer come from domain intuition rather than knowledge? Probe it again in the second phase before crediting it.
- What did they say in free text about how they think, or what they believe, or what they suspect they are faking? Those sentences are usually more valuable than the answers.
